\hypertarget{abstract}{%
\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

We study nonnegative integer heights on a finite graph, counted jointly
by total height and quadratic edge energy. Removing common translations
gives canonical minimum-zero profiles; an additional span mark separates
binary cuts from nested height configurations. We give explicit finite
examples in which equal Laplacian spectra, or equal complete binary
cut-volume censuses, coexist with unequal height populations. A third
comparison of regular graphs delays the first distinction to total
height four. For fixed real energy weight, we prove that a first
difference at total height r determines a zeta-factor exponent and a
power singularity at s=1/r in the ratio of associated prime products.
The construction is accompanied by standard-library exact software,
independent finite replay, a complete connected-graph census through six
vertices, and volume-truncated tree transfer methods. General
reconstruction and historical priority are left open. The purpose is an
exact account of what these particular observations preserve and where
their first lost correspondence becomes visible.

\hypertarget{question-and-background}{%
\section{Question and background}\label{question-and-background}}

A graph parameter can retain many numerical facts while forgetting the
correspondence between the configurations that produced them. The binary
cut-volume census is a useful example. It counts vertex subsets jointly
by cardinality and boundary size, so equality of this polynomial is
stronger than equality of a single energy moment. Nevertheless, it does
not retain which subsets are nested or which edges cross two of their
boundaries simultaneously. Integer heights provide a direct way to
observe those relationships.

The ingredients have established antecedents. Integral cut lattices
organize graph gradients modulo constants {[}BHN1997{]}. Bivariate Ising
polynomials organize binary configurations {[}AM2009{]}. Integer-valued
Gaussian models use quadratic graph energies {[}BPR2024{]}. Formal Euler
transforms reorganize coefficient data into cyclotomic factors
{[}Moree2005{]}. We use these ingredients with explicit marks and a
restricted inverse question. The paper does not claim a new origin for
the individual ingredients, nor does it claim that the proposed
scalar-height observations determine every graph.

There are two logically distinct constructions. First, a local
generating function records a finite graph's integer heights. Second, an
Euler product attaches that same local function to every rational prime.
The first determines graph-information content. The second supplies an
arithmetic organization and a precise comparison of singularities.
Keeping this distinction visible prevents a prime product from being
credited with information that it merely reencodes.

\hypertarget{local-populations-and-their-marks}{%
\section{Local populations and their
marks}\label{local-populations-and-their-marks}}

Let G be a finite connected simple loopless graph on vertices
0,\ldots,k-1, with k at least two. Write d\_i for its degrees and A\_ij
for its adjacency matrix. For a nonnegative integer vector a, put

\[
 |a|=\sum_i a_i,\qquad
 E_G(a)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E(G)}(a_i-a_j)^2.
\]

The unnormalized series and the fully marked normalized series are

\[
 L_G(x,q)=\sum_{a\ge0}x^{|a|}q^{E_G(a)},\qquad
 \mathcal B_G(x,q,z)=\sum_{\min a=0}x^{|a|}q^{E_G(a)}z^{\max a}.
\]

Every coefficient of x is a finite polynomial in q. Indeed, there are
only \(\binom{m+k-1}{k-1}\) nonnegative vectors of total height m. In
particular, these definitions are formal before any analytic parameter
is chosen. For 0 at most q at most one and absolute value of x below
one, comparison with \((1-|x|)^{-k}\) also gives analytic convergence.

\textbf{Proposition 1 (translation normalization).} With
\(B_G(x,q)=\mathcal B_G(x,q,1)\),

\[
 L_G(x,q)=\frac{B_G(x,q)}{1-x^k}.
\]

\textbf{Proof.} Every nonnegative vector has the unique form
\(a=b+t\mathbf1\), where \(t=\min a\) and \(\min b=0\). Edge
differences, and hence energy, are unchanged. Total height increases by
kt. Summing the common translation gives the geometric factor. This is a
formal identity with no convergence assumption. \(\square\)

The span mark records a genuine distinction. The binary cut polynomial

\[
 I_G(x,q)=\sum_{S\subseteq V(G)}x^{|S|}q^{|\partial S|}
\]

satisfies \(I_G=1+x^k+[z]\mathcal B_G\). Thus the full marked series
contains the binary census explicitly. The specialization z=1 merges
spans; a general recovery theorem for the binary sector from that
specialization is not established here.

\textbf{Proposition 2 (nested-boundary energy).} A normalized vector of
span h is equivalent to a chain of nonempty proper sets

\[
 S_h\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S_1,\qquad
 a=\sum_{t=1}^h\mathbf1_{S_t}.
\]

Repeated sets are permitted. If e(U,W) counts edges between disjoint
sets, then

\[
 |a|=\sum_t|S_t|,\qquad
 E_G(a)=\sum_t|\partial S_t|
       +2\sum_{r<t}e(S_t,V\setminus S_r).
\]

\textbf{Proof.} Take \(S_t=\{i:a_i\ge t\}\). Each coordinate is
recovered by counting its occupied thresholds, proving the bijection and
total-height identity. On an edge, every nonzero threshold difference
has the same sign. Expanding the square of their sum gives the
individual threshold crossings and twice every pair of simultaneous
crossings. For r below t, simultaneous crossing means that one endpoint
lies in S\_t and the other outside S\_r. Summing edges proves the
formula. \(\square\)

For two levels, this becomes
\(E(\mathbf1_S+\mathbf1_T)=|\partial S|+|\partial T|+2e(S,V\setminus T)\),
for nonempty S contained in proper T, allowing equality. The final term
explains why a distribution of isolated cuts can miss the multilevel
population.

\hypertarget{finite-visibility-and-reference-examples}{%
\section{Finite visibility and reference
examples}\label{finite-visibility-and-reference-examples}}

Put \(\ell_{G,m}(q)=[x^m]L_G(x,q)\). Splitting weak compositions by
their multiplicity patterns gives

\[
 \ell_{G,1}=\sum_iq^{d_i},\qquad
 \ell_{G,2}=\sum_iq^{4d_i}+\sum_{i<j}q^{d_i+d_j-2A_{ij}},
\]

\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \ell_{G,3}={}&\sum_iq^{9d_i}
 +\sum_{i\ne j}q^{4d_i+d_j-4A_{ij}}\\
 &+\sum_{i<j<t}q^{d_i+d_j+d_t-2(A_{ij}+A_{it}+A_{jt})}.
 \end{aligned}
\]

These formulas also delimit the meaning of a small-depth witness. Volume
one observes degrees. With degrees fixed, volume two observes the edge
degree-sum histogram. The current volume-three witness separates an
aggregated binary census by an ordered two-vertex height pattern; it is
not a claim that the graph energy contains a new three-body interaction.

All reference inputs are explicit. On vertices 0,\ldots,6, the first
pair has edge sets

\[
 \begin{aligned}
 E(G_2)&=\{01,02,03,04,12,35,36\},\\
 E(H_2)&=\{01,02,03,04,15,16,56\}.
 \end{aligned}
\]

Their degree multisets and Laplacian characteristic polynomials agree.
The latter is

\[
 t^7-14t^6+73t^5-178t^4+209t^3-112t^2+21t.
\]

The unique degree-four vertex lies in a triangle in the first graph and
not the second, so they are nonisomorphic. Direct coefficient
enumeration gives first difference

\[
 \ell_{G_2,2}-\ell_{H_2,2}=2q^2(1-q)^3(1+q).
\]

The second pair has edge sets

\[
 \begin{aligned}
 E(G_3)&=\{03,04,06,12,13,23,24,35\},\\
 E(H_3)&=\{01,04,06,15,16,26,36,45\}.
 \end{aligned}
\]

Its graphs have the same complete binary cut-volume polynomial:
enumeration of all 128 subsets agrees at every pair of subset size and
boundary size. Their unique degree-four vertices have respectively one
and two leaf neighbors, proving nonisomorphism. The local coefficients
agree through volume two, while

\[
 \ell_{G_3,3}-\ell_{H_3,3}
 =q^3(1-q)(1-q^3)^2(1-q^4)^2.
\]

This entire discrepancy occurs in span two. Span one agrees by the
binary-census equality; span three agrees by the equal degree multisets.
Independent enumeration and polynomial multiplication check the
factorization. Both differences are strictly positive on the open
interval between zero and one.

A third pair, the three-dimensional cube and the eight-vertex Mobius
ladder, consists of triangle-free cubic graphs. Their local coefficients
agree through volume three and first differ at volume four by
\(2q^4(1-q^2)^4\). That difference is binary. The example illustrates
delayed visibility after matching regular low-order data; it does not
duplicate the binary-census equality of the second pair.

\hypertarget{arithmetic-assembly-and-the-comparison-theorem}{%
\section{Arithmetic assembly and the comparison
theorem}\label{arithmetic-assembly-and-the-comparison-theorem}}

For a positive integer n, let v\_p(n) be its p-adic valuation. Define

\[
 \mathcal E_G(n_1,\ldots,n_k)
 =\sum_pE_G(v_p(n_1),\ldots,v_p(n_k)),
\]

\[
 Z_G(s,q)=\sum_{n_1,\ldots,n_k\ge1}
 \frac{q^{\mathcal E_G(\mathbf n)}}{(n_1\cdots n_k)^s}.
\]

For fixed q in the closed unit interval and real part of s greater than
one, absolute convergence and unique factorization give
\(Z_G(s,q)=\prod_pL_G(p^{-s},q)\). The construction is multiplicative
across primes, while each prime's local factor retains the graph
interaction among its exponent coordinates.

\textbf{Theorem 3 (first-discrepancy visibility).} Fix q between zero
and one. Suppose two same-order graphs satisfy
\(\ell_{G,m}(q)=\ell_{H,m}(q)\) for m below r, and
\(\delta=\ell_{G,r}(q)-\ell_{H,r}(q)\ne0\). Then their ratio Euler
product converges for real s greater than 1/r and equals

\[
 R_{G,H}(s,q)=\zeta(rs)^\delta K_{G,H}(s,q),
\]

where K has a finite positive nonzero limit at s=1/r and is holomorphic
and nonzero in a complex neighborhood of that point. Consequently

\[
 R_{G,H}(s,q)\sim K_{G,H}(1/r,q)(rs-1)^{-\delta}
 \quad(s\downarrow1/r).
\]

\textbf{Proof.} Constant terms are one, so cancellation of the lower
local coefficients gives \(L_G/L_H=1+\delta x^r+O(x^{r+1})\). The ratio
Euler product therefore has an absolutely summable logarithm for real s
greater than 1/r. Every local factor is positive there. Multiplication
by \((1-x^r)^\delta\) removes the first discrepancy, yielding
\(1+O(x^{r+1})\). Thus

\[
 K_{G,H}(s,q)=\prod_p(1-p^{-rs})^\delta
              \frac{L_G(p^{-s},q)}{L_H(p^{-s},q)}
\]

converges for real s greater than 1/(r+1). Near s=1/r, finitely many
small-prime factors are nonzero by positivity and continuity; the
remaining tail has a normally convergent logarithm. This proves the
local complex statement. The zeta Euler product proves the factorization
on the right of 1/r, and \(\zeta(1+t)\sim1/t\) gives the asymptotic.
\(\square\)

At q=1/2, the first two examples yield exponents 3/32 at s=1/2 and
11025/262144 at s=1/3. The cube comparison yields 81/2048 at s=1/4.
Nonintegral exponents give power or branch singularities, not ordinary
poles. The quotient is initially a quotient of convergent positive
series on s\textgreater1; its ratio Euler product supplies the extension
used in the theorem. No global meromorphic-continuation claim follows.

\hypertarget{synchronization-and-its-singular-limit}{%
\section{Synchronization and its singular
limit}\label{synchronization-and-its-singular-limit}}

Connectedness implies zero energy exactly at constant heights. Hence
\(Z_G(s,0)=\zeta(ks)\), whereas \(Z_G(s,1)=\zeta(s)^k\). More generally,
the first local coefficient is \(\alpha_G(q)=\sum_iq^{d_i}\), and
removal of the linear Euler term gives

\[
 Z_G(s,q)=\zeta(s)^{\alpha_G(q)}H_G(s,q),
\]

with H regular and positive near real s=1. Every fixed positive q
therefore restores an s=1 divergence, while exact synchronization q=0
leaves the finite value zeta(k) there. The order of these two limits
matters.

Let d be minimum degree and b the number of vertices attaining it. If q
tends to zero and real s tends down to one with
\(q^d\log(1/(s-1))\to c\), then

\[
 \frac{Z_G(s,q)}{\zeta(ks)}\longrightarrow e^{bc}.
\]

To justify this limit, use translation-normalized local factors and
subtract their common linear term. Their tails are uniformly bounded by
a summable multiple of p\^{}\{-2s\} near s=1, using the weak-composition
coefficient bound. The normalized product tends to one by dominated
convergence. Finally \(\alpha_G(q)=bq^d+O(q^{d+1})\) supplies the
exponential limit. This argument describes a boundary layer in the
declared real domain, independently of any global continuation question.

The first positive energy has a separate combinatorial interpretation.
Integer coarea bounds energy below by the sum of threshold cut sizes,
hence by mincut times span. Equality at the minimum positive energy
forces a binary minimum-cut indicator. The first energy coefficient of
\(\log(Z_G/\zeta(ks))\) is consequently a sum of prime-zeta functions
\(\sum_{|\partial S|=\lambda}P(|S|s)\), counting both cut orientations.
This links cut side sizes to an analytic coefficient without claiming
that minimum cuts identify the whole graph.

\hypertarget{computational-scope-and-open-structural-questions}{%
\section{Computational scope and open structural
questions}\label{computational-scope-and-open-structural-questions}}

The released computational core uses integer and rational arithmetic. It
supplies weak-composition counts, marked sectors, exact Laplacian
characteristic polynomials, formal Euler exponents with independent
recomposition, constrained original-label rank and unrank, forest
message passing, root-of-unity residue numerators, weighted hard
contraction and primitive-profile inversion. A finite certificate
records a graph source, coefficient range and declared comparison;
replay enumerates profiles independently and checks the normalization
and threshold identities. These are software-level exact checks, not
proof-assistant certificates for the entire implementation.

An exhaustive enumeration of all connected labeled simple graphs through
six vertices, quotiented by degree-respecting permutations, yields
respectively 1, 1, 2, 6, 21 and 112 isomorphism classes. For order six,
local coefficients through volumes one, two and three distinguish
respectively 68, 100 and 112 classes. This is a complete finite census
with an explicit generation boundary. It does not imply completeness at
larger orders. In particular, the seven- and eight-vertex examples
illustrate why visibility depth must remain part of the specification.

The main inverse problem asks whether the fully marked series identifies
a graph in a declared class. A negative answer should describe a
transformation or an exact pair preserving all heights, rather than
merely a long agreeing prefix. A complementary question asks whether
nonisomorphic pairs can have first-separation depth tending to infinity.
Both questions concern scalar marks. If every vertex receives an
independent variable, labeled adjacency is recovered already from the
one- and two-coordinate coefficients, making that unrestricted version
much easier.

The prime lift adds an analytic hierarchy to these local questions. It
does not add graph information: coefficients at prime powers recover the
local coefficients, and local coefficients determine the Euler product.
Its contribution is to turn a finite first discrepancy into a controlled
singularity, allowing relational depth and arithmetic location to be
studied together.
